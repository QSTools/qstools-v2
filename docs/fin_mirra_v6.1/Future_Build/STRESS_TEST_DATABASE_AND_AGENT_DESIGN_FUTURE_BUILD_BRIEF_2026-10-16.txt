1. WHY THIS DOCUMENT EXISTS
===============================================================================
The smoke test has used one or two staff and a handful of assets. Before launch
the owner wants the tool pushed with a whole synthetic business, then scaled
until something gives, because a silently wrong number on a client's real
financials is the one failure that cannot be allowed at launch. The discussion
then ran on into the database, security, the AI agent and load, because they
depend on the same thing: knowing the exact shape of every record the tool
stores and every number it calculates. Recording them together stops them
being re-derived.

3. THE SYNTHETIC-BUSINESS STRESS TEST
===============================================================================
3.1 Why bottom-up matters (and its limit)
  Earlier synthetic staff and assets were NOT added to the P&L, so Model
  Readiness stayed blocked on the business cost benchmark reconciliation
  (kickoff doc; tracker section 17, C16 - expected then, no code change). A
  P&L built from the staff and asset records should clear that block. That is
  itself a test: if Model Readiness stays blocked on a bottom-up business, it is
  a finding.
  LIMIT: if the generator uses the tool's own formulas to build the P&L, the
  cost reconciliation passes trivially and proves nothing about those formulas.
  The generator and its expected values must come from an INDEPENDENT script
  written from CALCULATION_MAP_v6_0.txt (Labour section 3 for hours and
  on-costs), not from the app's code. Expected totals are produced outside the
  app, then compared to what the app shows.
  Also carry the known caveat: the owner or director's own labour cost explained
  most of the earlier P&L-versus-tool labour gap (Business Outcome brief s5,
  owner-remuneration design item). Decide how the generator treats it.

3.2 Scenario A (first business) - proposed from the discussion
  10 staff: 7 productive, 3 non-productive.
  7 assets: 4 productive, 3 non-productive. The owner said "seven assets and
  three non-productive"; the 4/3 productive split was agreed in conversation.
  P&L built bottom-up per 3.1: labour and on-costs from staff, asset interest
  and depreciation from assets, overheads layered on (rent, insurance, admin,
  etc.), COGS for materials.
  NOT YET SPECIFIED: revenue and charge-out rates (the P&L Sales figure), the
  working groups and which staff and assets sit in which group, overhead pool
  assignments, and the rate builder set-up. Cost Allocation groups, pool
  assignments and Rate Builder calculators are inputs the user builds, not
  imports, and the seed must contain them or the chain cannot run.

3.3 Scale ladder and the $100M arithmetic
  Proposed ladder: 10 staff, then the same shape at 50, then further (100, then
  larger) until something breaks. Keep the shape constant until a failure is
  understood; only then change the business.
  Scale check from smoke-test figures (VERIFIED in the smoke test brief: one
  productive person at 40 h/wk, 85 percent productivity, about 1,414 recovered
  hours, $100 an hour, about $141,000 revenue). On labour alone:
      100 staff, about 70 productive  -> about $10 million a year
      about 700 productive staff       -> about $100 million a year
  So the "100 staff" step is a $10M business, not $100M. A $100M business is
  either roughly 700 to 1,000 staff at that rate, or fewer staff at higher
  rates, or an asset-heavy shape. The shape matters more than the number
  (owner agreed). Two $100M routes to test, once the same-shape ladder holds:
    HIGH HEADCOUNT, LOWER RATE   (labour-hire or trades style)
    HIGH-VALUE ASSETS, FEW STAFF (plant-hire or specialist asset style)
  They stress different paths: staff list and labour aggregation versus asset
  allocation and Rate Builder asset pricing.
  Exposed risk worth watching at scale: totals that silently differ from the
  sum of visible rows (precedent BO-1, BO-11 in the Business Outcome work), and
  floating-point drift over thousands of line items. Both are hypotheses to
  test, not observed faults.

3.4 Seeding method and hazards
  There is NO import path for staff or assets today. The proposed route is a
  JSON file written into localStorage keys by a console script, then a reload.
  - Run it ONLY on the isolated instance (localhost:3004, its own localStorage
    origin, used for the smoke test). Never against the real :3000 dataset.
  - Back up first. The Step F precedent: restore means putting back ALL keys,
    not one (pre-flip backup was 19 keys, 96,213 characters).
  - First get the real shapes from the tool, not from memory: run the existing
    localStorage dump (smoke test brief section 0, method 3, filters qs_tools_
    and qstools_ prefixes) on the current dataset, then read the storage files.
  - VERIFIED names so far (tracker): lib/storage/assetStorage.js, with
    qs_tools_asset_state as the editor DRAFT and a separate saved-list key (the
    tracker line is cut off; do not guess it); qs_tools_business_setup_v1.
  - UNVERIFIED: the Labour storage file name, its key and record shape, the
    Cost Allocation, Rate Builder, P&L and Balance Sheet keys, ID format.
  - Hazards that make a naive write look populated but malformed: the editor
    draft versus the saved list in asset storage; the has_loaded guard (a save
    effect firing before load overwrites real data with the empty default);
    a storage or selector layer that REBUILDS an object and silently drops any
    field not in its list (conventions bug class b); calculated fields that are
    recomputed on read. A seed field that does not survive a round trip will
    look fine and give a wrong answer.

3.5 What the second and later businesses should deliberately cover
  Smoke brief section 10 logs these as untested: multiple working groups
  sharing one overhead pool; an asset or staff member shared or split across
  two groups; genuine product or unit mode with real unit-driver data (Step F
  is the first real test of that). The Opening Hours versus Labour coverage
  gap is a confirmed missing check and stays out of scope. The different
  businesses should hit the first three on purpose.

3.6 UI performance pass (separate from correctness)
  A localStorage seed proves data shape and calculation. It does not prove the
  screens stay responsive. NOT OBSERVED YET: it is a hypothesis that one edit
  re-renders every staff row, and that long unvirtualised lists slow down at
  hundreds of rows. Method: load the seeded data, then use the real pages with
  the React DevTools Profiler (installed) and the Performance tab; record which
  components re-render when one staff rate is changed, and the time to respond.
  Measure the 10-staff run first and record the others as ratios to it. Define
  "too slow" before the run, not after.

3.7 The pack is also the regression baseline
  Keep the generator, the seed files and the independently computed expected
  values. Re-run them after every change that touches shared calculation or
  storage code: the Business Modelling redesign (1b), units (4), purchase
  order merge (4b), server migration (2 and 3), and the database (5). The same
  seed compared before and after migration is a direct test of migration
  integrity. This is why the pack is worth building properly once.

3.8 Provisional shape freeze
  The owner's expectation that this pass fixes every entry point is right for
  the shapes that exist today. But units, 1b and 4b will add or change shapes
  before the database step. Treat the entry-point register as provisional
  until step 4b is closed, and re-audit stable record IDs at the start of
  step 5 (the 2026-10-16 go-to-market brief, section 7, already says this).

5. DATABASE DESIGN RULES
===============================================================================
5.1 Decided by the owner (Section 2): live before launch with no localStorage
    migration (D-5); one multi-tenant database (D-6); every calculated number
    stored (D-7).
    Consequence of D-5 to note: staging and the owner's own datasets that sit
    only in browser storage are not carried over. The JSON seed path (Section
    3.4) is how a dataset is loaded again.
5.2 Tenant isolation and encryption - requirement versus open choice
  REQUIREMENT: a tenant key (business or user id) on every row that holds user
  data, every query scoped to it, and row-level security enforced inside the
  database as the backstop, so a bug in application code cannot return another
  business's rows. Day one, not a hardening pass.
  LAYERS (explained in discussion):
    in transit (TLS)              standard, expected from any host
    at rest (disk level)          applied by most managed database hosts, free
                                  in performance terms. VERIFY for whichever
                                  provider is chosen; do not assume.
    field level (own key)         a deliberate choice; the database can no
                                  longer filter, sort, index or sum the value,
                                  so work moves into the application and slows.
  The owner's first instinct was "encrypt everything". The reasoning given was:
  encrypting everything at field level would hurt exactly the consistent,
  queryable data that Cost Allocation and Rate Builder need, and the P&L and
  Balance Sheet are already covered by TLS plus at-rest plus row-level
  security. Proposed target: that baseline for the whole database, and
  field-level encryption for a short, deliberate list of the most sensitive
  fields that are rarely queried in bulk. NOT DECIDED. Derived outputs (Business
  Outcome and the modelled layer) can be as sensitive as raw inputs and belong
  on the list for consideration. Encrypting only the user identifier was
  considered and rejected: the data identifies the business anyway, and the app
  needs the key on every query.
  Most real breaches do not break encryption. They get the running application
  to hand data over (a flaw, a leaked key, stolen credentials), which is why
  query scoping and key handling matter as much as the cipher.
  RECOMMENDED before the schema is fixed: put the field-level list in front of
  someone who has secured financial SaaS data, because the stakes are high and
  this was reasoned from first principles, not from experience.
5.3 Working schema model (proposed, owner agreed it fits)
    BASELINE   what the business is today: staff, assets, Cost Allocation
               groups, Rate Builder calculators, imported P&L and Balance
               Sheet. Mostly slow-changing "dimension" style records, each
               with an ID and descriptive attributes.
    MODELLED   the scenario layer: Business Modelling levers and saved
               scenarios, referencing the same IDs as the baseline and holding
               a parallel set of numbers. Check against the 1b redesign brief
               (scratch overlay) before locking this layer.
    LIVE       operational events over time: purchase orders, timekeeping,
               and each new P&L or Balance Sheet import. "Fact" style, time
               stamped, referencing the baseline IDs.
  Dimension versus fact in one line: a dimension is a thing (a staff member,
  an asset, a group); a fact is a number about a thing on a date.
5.4 Stored outputs
  Rule D-7 means each calculation module's output contract becomes stored data,
  not only a value passed between hooks. Implications to design (not decided):
  snapshots keyed by business, date and scenario; a recalculation on any change
  to baseline or modelled inputs; each snapshot stamped with what it was
  computed from, so a stale number can be detected; and what "every level"
  means in rows (every intermediate node, or each module's output contract).
  ASSUMED, check: the shipped calculation trace already gives every number a
  node identity, which may be the natural unit to store. The stored number must
  be written by the same calculation code the app uses (server side after steps
  2 and 3), never re-derived elsewhere, so there is one source of truth.
5.5 Stable IDs
  Every record needs a stable ID and references by ID, not array position.
  Several shapes were recalled as already having IDs; that was from memory of
  the briefs, NOT audited. Audit coverage at the start of step 5.
5.6 Operating while live
  With real users, "never lose their data" becomes a database duty: additive,
  versioned migrations; back up before each; change a field's meaning only
  across more than one release; point-in-time recovery decided with the host.
  For code-only changes a user in a session simply gets the new version on
  reload. This replaces the localStorage versioning advice given in discussion,
  which no longer applies once D-5 holds.
5.7 Purchase orders and timekeeping
  The owner's framing: the tool sets a baseline and a model; purchase orders,
  timekeeping and invoices are how the business is controlled afterwards, and
  what returns to re-run through the core is the periodic P&L and Balance Sheet
  import, which already has defined injection points. So the live operational
  data may sit in its own tables, and the core baseline database need not wait
  for them provided their output lands in the same P&L-shaped feed. This
  CONFLICTS with the existing build order, where step 4b places the purchase
  order merge BEFORE the database so the schema is designed once. Open
  question Q-3.

6. THE LOAD QUESTION
===============================================================================
Today all compute runs in each user's browser and storage is per browser, so
many users do not contend over anything shared. That changes at build order
steps 2 and 3: the calculation dispatcher runs on the server (Vercel in
production), and the database adds shared storage. Server load then matters,
and step 6 of the existing build order already covers it.
Method when the time comes: a load tool (k6 suggested; Artillery and JMeter are
alternatives) scripted against the real endpoints on a deployed environment,
not localhost, ramping virtual users and reading response time and error rate.
Until then, the P&L import API route is the only server endpoint with real
work. Per D-9 and D-10 this comes after the database step and runs as
background work, not as a launch blocker.

7. THE EXPLAINER AGENT
===============================================================================
7.1 Role (D-8): read and explain, never compute. It fetches stored,
    already-calculated outcomes and explains, compares and points out patterns
    in plain language.
7.2 Shape: the agent calls a fixed set of defined tool functions (for example,
    get a business's Business Outcome summary, a staff member's true cost, the
    current Cost Allocation groups). Each returns structured JSON read from the
    stored tables, scoped to the one business by the same isolation as 5.2. It
    does not write its own database queries from the conversation.
7.3 Hypotheticals: the owner would allow modelling a what-if if clearly
    labelled as not the truth. Proposed implementation (not confirmed): the
    agent triggers Business Modelling's own lever calculation and explains the
    result, rather than doing the arithmetic itself. Either way no hypothetical
    figure is shown as an actual.
7.4 Logging: proposed, owner has not commented. Keep a record of what was asked
    and what data was returned, for trust and for client compliance asks.
7.5 Dependencies: the stored outputs (5.4), tenant isolation (5.2), the
    explanation text already planned on calculation nodes (AI brief decision D1,
    result_interpretation). Without stored outputs the agent would have to
    approximate, which D-8 forbids.
7.6 Check before building: the 2 March 2026 Xero developer terms on AI/ML use
    of API data (go-to-market brief, Q2) against an agent that reads figures
    derived from Xero data.
